Where:

 $$  Q=outlet capacity(m^{3}/s) $$ 

 $$  A\ =cross sectional area(m^{2}) $$ 

 $$  g\quad=\quad acceleration due to gravity(9.81m/s^{2}) $$ 

h = depth of water above outlet pipe's invert (m)

 $$  S_{ 冮 }=slope of outlet pipe(m/m) $$

## Losses

The total energy loss through the pipe may be computed as:

 $$ \mathbf{K_{L}}=\mathbf{k_{t}}+\mathbf{k_{e}}+\mathbf{k_{f}}+\mathbf{k_{b}}+\mathbf{k_{o}} $$ 

Where:

 $$  k_{t}=trash rack loss factor $$ 

 $$ \mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{e}}\;=\;\mathrm{e n t r a n c e l o s s f a c t o r} $$ 

 $$ \mathrm{k_{f}~=~friction~loss~factor} $$ 

 $$  k_{b}=bend loss factor $$ 

 $$  k_{o}=outlet loss factor $$ 

## Trash Rack Loss Factor

The loss factor at a trash rack can be approximated using the following equation:

 $$ \mathbf{k_{t}}=1.45-0.45(\mathbf{a n/a g})\cdot(\mathbf{a n/a g})^{2} $$ 

Where:

an = net open area between the rack bars

ag = gross area of the rack and supports

One approach is to assume that 50% of the rack area is blocked when estimating the maximum potential losses at the rack. Also, calculate the maximum outlet capacity assuming no blockage. It is recommended minimum and maximum outlet capacities be checked to ensure that the installation will function adequately, under both possible operating scenarios, in the field.

## Entrance Loss Factor

An entrance loss factor can be developed by taking the orifice equation, rearranging its terms, and recognizing that the depth term in the equation is actually the sum of the velocity head and the head loss:

 $$ \mathbf{k_{e}}=\mathbf{1}/(\mathbf{C_{d}})^{2}\cdot\mathbf{1} $$ 

Where:

 $$  C_{d}\qquad\quad=orifice discharge coefficient $$ 

## Friction Loss Factor

The pipe friction loss factor for a pipe flowing full is expressed as:

 $$ \mathbf{k_{f}}=\mathbf{f}\left(\mathbf{L}/\mathbf{D}\right) $$ 

Where:

f = Darcy-Weisbach friction loss coefficient which, under simplifying assumptions, can be expressed as a function of Manning's n

 $$ \mathbf{f}=185~(\mathbf{n}^{2}/\mathbf{D}^{1/3}) $$ 

Bend Loss Factor

Bend losses in a closed conduit are a function of bend radius, pipe diameter, and the deflection angle at the bend. For 90 degree bends having a radius at least twice the pipe diameter, a value of  $ k_{b} = k_{90} = 0.2 $ is often used. For bends having other than 90 degree bends, the bend loss factor can be calculated using the following equation:

## Outlet Loss Factor

Virtually no recovery of velocity head occurs where the pipe freely discharges into the atmosphere or is submerged under water. As a result, unless a specially shaped flared outlet is provided, it is safe to assume that  $ k_{0} = 1.0 $.
