## J Calculation of k

For the sampled sequence  $ x_{1}, \ldots, x_{T} $ to be predicted, we denote the query vectors of the target positions as  $ q_{1}, \ldots, q_{T} $. We then compute the matrix  $ \hat{\mathbf{Q}} = (\mathbf{q}_{1} - \overline{\mathbf{q}}, \ldots, \mathbf{q}_{T} - \overline{\mathbf{q}}) $ by subtracting the mean vector,  $ \overline{q} $, from each query vector. Subsequently, we determine the M directions,  $ v_{1}, \ldots, v_{M} $, that correspond to the M largest variation directions for the centralized query vectors  $ \hat{q}_{1}, \ldots, \hat{q}_{T} $. The  $ i^{th} $ direction,  $ v_{i} $, is chosen to maximize the variance of the projection of the centralized query vectors onto it, while also being orthogonal to the previously chosen directions,  $ v_{1}, \ldots, v_{i-1} $. This process can be formalized as follows:

 $$ \begin{aligned}\mathbf{v}_{1}&=\underset{\|\mathbf{v}\|=1}{\arg\max}\operatorname{Var}\left\{\mathbf{v}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}\right\},\\\mathbf{v}_{2}&=\underset{\|\mathbf{v}\|=1,\mathbf{v}\perp\mathbf{v}_{1}}{\arg\max}\operatorname{Var}\left\{\mathbf{v}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}\right\},\end{aligned} $$ 

 $$ \mathbf{v}_{M}=\underset{\|\mathbf{v}\|=1,\mathbf{v}\perp\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}\perp\mathbf{v}_{M-1}}{\arg\max}\operatorname{Var}\left\{\mathbf{v}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}\right\}. $$ 

We define  $ \sigma_{i} $ as the square root of the variance of the projection of  $ \hat{Q} $ onto the  $ i^{th} $ direction, i.e.,

 $$ 

\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\left\{\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{Q}}\right\}}.

 $$ 

To derive features  $ \hat{k}_{s} $, we project the key vector k onto the directions  $ v_{1}, \ldots, v_{M} $ and scale the projections by the corresponding standard deviations  $ \sigma_{1}, \ldots, \sigma_{M} $. Each feature,  $ \hat{k}_{i} $, is thus calculated as  $ \sigma_{i}v_{i}^{T}k $.

We further examine the influence of M on the prediction confusion matrix, Confusion $ _{j}^{pred} $, as depicted in Figure 14. Given the similarity in outcomes for various M, we settle on a value of M = 10 for computation of Confusion $ _{j}^{pred} $.

## K Calculation of Confusion $ _{ij} $

To gauge the true degree of confusion between categories i and k for a given model, we suggest utilizing the Confusion $ _{ij} $ metric:

First, we procure all test samples  $ x_{t} $ bearing true labels i or k. We then obtain the probabilities  $ p_{i}^{t} $ and  $ p_{j}^{t} $ yielded by the model for categories i and k, respectively, on these samples. These probabilities are normalized to a total of 1. Essentially, we derive a classifier f that delivers the probabilities  $ p_{i}^{t} $ and  $ p_{j}^{t} $ for the categories i and k respectively, on the test samples  $ x_{t} $. By calculating the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC) value of this classifier f, we get the degree of confusion between category i and k, termed as  $ Confusion_{ij} $.



The computed Confusionij is a value that never exceeds 1. The closer Confusionij approximates 1, the less pronounced the confusion, and vice versa.

We use the above metric instead of directly analyzing the output labels of the model because previous work has indicated the issue of insufficient output probability calibration in ICL (Zhao et al., 2021), which is greatly affected by factors such as sample ordering and model preferences for specific label words. By leveraging our defined degree of confusion, Confusion $ _{ij} $, we can implicitly alleviate the disturbances arising from insufficient probability calibration on the output labels. This allows for a more accurate representation of the model's degree of confusion for different categories, mitigating the impact of randomness.
