The peat was too acidic to grow rice, resulting in the failure of all crops and abandonment of the project in 1998 (Commitante et al., 2003). The land now lies mostly unused.

This project resulted in widespread destruction. In 1997, the dry peat combined with dead wood debris and dry season drought led to uncontrollable fires in the area which burned for six months (Commitante et al., 2003). The resulting haze blanketed several countries in Southeast Asia, causing failure of crops, the death of over 500 people, illness to 20 million more, and caused massive economic loss (Brown, 1998).

Studies quantifying the carbon release as a result of the 1997 fires found that as much as 2.57 Gt (gigaton,  $ 10^{9} $ tonnes or  $ 10^{12} $ kg) of carbon was released into the atmosphere, the equivalence of 40% the mean annual global carbon emissions and the largest increase in atmospheric  $ CO_{2} $ levels in recorded history (Page et al., 2002). As a result of burning and subsidence, approximately a half meter of peat was lost (Page et al., 2002). Areas which have burnt previously become further vulnerable to fire (Woods, 1989; Langner and Siegert, 2009), and so this area now experiences annual fires during the dry season (Commitante et al., 2003).
